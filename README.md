# Official Frameworks for Building Certified AI Solutions

> Curated list of government-mandated regulations, legally binding treaties, and international standards body frameworks specifically governing AI development, deployment, and certification. Last updated: May 2026.

---

## 1. EU AI Act

| Field | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Version** | Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 |
| **Jurisdiction** | European Union (+ extraterritorial reach) |
| **URL** | https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32024R1689 |

The EU AI Act is the world's most comprehensive legally binding AI regulation, adopting a risk-based tiered approach (unacceptable, high, limited, minimal risk). It entered into force on 1 August 2024, with enforcement rolling out in stages: prohibited AI practices banned from February 2025, general-purpose AI (GPAI) obligations active from August 2025, and full high-risk system rules phased through August 2027. It applies to any organization placing AI systems on the EU market, regardless of where they are based. High-risk systems require conformity assessments, technical documentation, human oversight, and registration in an EU database. GPAI model providers — including large language models — must maintain training data summaries, implement safety policies, and notify the EU AI Office of systemic risks. The Act is now the de facto global compliance anchor: organizations outside the EU are building products to EU AI Act standards to maintain market access.

## 2. NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF)

| Field | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Version** | AI RMF 1.0 (January 2023); updated guidance released March 2025 |
| **Jurisdiction** | United States (voluntary; referenced globally) |
| **URL** | https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework |

The NIST AI RMF, published by the U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology, is the primary voluntary framework for managing AI risk across the full system lifecycle. It is structured around four core functions — Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage — and is designed to be sector-agnostic and technology-neutral. Although not legally enforceable, it is referenced by U.S. federal agencies, regulators (FTC, FDA, SEC), and international standards bodies as a baseline; federal contractors are increasingly expected to align with it. The March 2025 update strengthened requirements around model provenance, data integrity, and third-party model risk, reflecting the shift toward organizations relying on external AI components. NIST has also initiated work on Control Overlays for Securing AI Systems (COSAIS), building AI-specific security controls on top of NIST SP 800-53, with a first public draft expected in early 2026. The AI RMF is widely used as a technical companion framework for EU AI Act compliance and maps directly to ISO/IEC 42001.

## 3. ISO/IEC 42001:2023 — AI Management System Standard

| Field | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Version** | ISO/IEC 42001:2023 (first edition) |
| **Jurisdiction** | International (ISO/IEC member countries) |
| **URL** | https://www.iso.org/standard/81230.html |

ISO/IEC 42001 is the world's first certifiable AI management system standard, establishing requirements for organizations to design, implement, maintain, and continually improve an AI governance framework. It follows the familiar ISO high-level structure (also used by ISO 27001 and ISO 9001), covering leadership commitment, risk assessment, data governance, transparency, and continuous improvement. Unlike the NIST AI RMF, which is prescriptive guidance, ISO 42001 enables third-party certification — organizations can be audited and awarded formal certification demonstrating AI governance maturity. Certification auditors must themselves qualify under the companion standard ISO/IEC 42006:2025. Adoption is accelerating rapidly in 2025–2026 as organizations use it as a practical bridge between the EU AI Act and NIST frameworks, since crosswalk mappings exist between all three. It is increasingly cited in procurement requirements and regulatory conformity pathways.

## 4. ISO/IEC 42005:2025 — AI System Impact Assessment

| Field | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Version** | ISO/IEC 42005:2025 (first edition, published May 2025) |
| **Jurisdiction** | International (ISO/IEC member countries) |
| **URL** | https://www.iso.org/standard/42005 |

ISO/IEC 42005 is the first international standard dedicated specifically to AI system impact assessment (AIIA), providing structured guidance for evaluating how AI systems affect individuals, groups, and society across the full lifecycle — from design through decommissioning. It is a process standard (not a certification scheme), intended to complement ISO/IEC 42001 by adding system-level accountability to organizational governance. The standard mandates lifecycle-oriented assessments covering social, economic, environmental, ethical, and governance dimensions, and requires organizations to integrate findings into existing risk management processes rather than treat them as standalone tasks. Its publication is strategically timed: the EU AI Act explicitly requires impact assessments for high-risk AI systems, and ISO/IEC 42005 serves as evidence of due diligence in regulatory conformity. Organizations implementing it alongside ISO/IEC 42001 now have a comprehensive, internationally recognized governance and impact toolkit.

## 5. ISO/IEC 42006:2025 — Requirements for AI Audit and Certification Bodies

| Field | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Version** | ISO/IEC 42006:2025 (published 2025) |
| **Jurisdiction** | International (ISO/IEC member countries) |
| **URL** | https://www.iso.org/standard/42006 |

ISO/IEC 42006 defines the competence, consistency, and impartiality requirements for bodies that audit and certify organizations against ISO/IEC 42001. It ensures that AI auditors are qualified, follow consistent methodologies, and can produce credible, comparable certification outcomes. Without this standard, ISO/IEC 42001 certifications would lack a shared assurance baseline, undermining their credibility. The standard addresses AI-specific auditing challenges — including assessing opaque models, evaluating data governance, and verifying ongoing monitoring — that general management system auditor frameworks do not cover. Published in 2025, it operationalizes the AI certification ecosystem, enabling accreditation bodies to begin approving AI certification auditors at scale. It is a foundational enabler for the entire ISO AI standards family to function as a credible, market-facing certification regime.

## 6. Council of Europe Framework Convention on AI (CETS No. 225)

| Field | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Version** | CETS No. 225 (adopted 17 May 2024; opened for signature 5 September 2024; entered into force 1 November 2025) |
| **Jurisdiction** | International (Council of Europe + non-member signatories including USA, EU, UK, Japan, Canada, Australia) |
| **URL** | https://www.coe.int/en/web/artificial-intelligence/the-framework-convention-on-artificial-intelligence |

The Council of Europe Framework Convention on Artificial Intelligence and Human Rights, Democracy and the Rule of Law (CETS No. 225) is the world's first legally binding international treaty governing AI. Drafted between 2022 and 2024 with participation from all 46 CoE member states, the EU, and 11 non-member nations, it entered into force on 1 November 2025 following ratification by the UK, France, Norway, and others. The treaty applies a technology-neutral, risk-based approach to the entire AI lifecycle and sets overarching obligations on legality, transparency, accountability, non-discrimination, access to remedies, and independent oversight. Notably, it is open to any country in the world to ratify — a design choice that positions it as a potential global baseline treaty. Implementation is overseen by a Conference of the Parties. For EU member states, compliance is largely achieved through the EU AI Act. The treaty is now emerging alongside a draft UN AI Global Accord as a cornerstone of the international AI governance architecture.

## 7. OECD Recommendation on Artificial Intelligence (OECD AI Principles)

| Field | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Version** | Original 2019; revised and updated 2024 |
| **Jurisdiction** | International (OECD member countries + adherents; 69 countries tracked) |
| **URL** | https://oecd.ai/en/ai-principles |

The OECD AI Principles are the first intergovernmental standard on AI, originally adopted in May 2019 and updated in 2024 to reflect advances in generative AI and agentic systems. They establish five core principles — inclusive growth, human-centered values, transparency, robustness, and accountability — that have become the conceptual grammar underpinning most subsequent national and international AI frameworks, including the EU AI Act, NIST AI RMF, and the CoE Convention. While non-binding, they are politically significant: adherent governments commit to implementing them in national policy, and the OECD AI Policy Observatory tracks implementation across more than 1,000 policy initiatives globally. The 2024 update strengthened provisions on GPAI models, frontier model safety, and transparency. The principles function as a normative anchor for international alignment, with the G7 Hiroshima AI Process, G20, and UNESCO frameworks all explicitly mapping to them.

## 8. G7 Hiroshima AI Process — Code of Conduct for AI Developers

| Field | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Version** | Launched October 2023 (Hiroshima Summit); updated 2025 under Italian G7 Presidency |
| **Jurisdiction** | G7 nations (Canada, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, UK, USA + EU) |
| **URL** | https://www.g7italy.it/en/artificial-intelligence/ |

The G7 Hiroshima AI Process established a voluntary Code of Conduct for organizations developing advanced AI systems, with a particular focus on frontier and foundation models. The 11-point Code covers pre-deployment safety testing, reporting of AI incidents and vulnerabilities, investment in cybersecurity, transparency about AI-generated content (watermarking), and research collaboration on AI safety. While non-binding, the Code reflects policy convergence among the world's leading AI-producing nations and has become a reference point for national AI safety institute mandates in the UK, Japan, USA, and EU. Under the 2025 Italian G7 Presidency, the Process was extended and connected to the broader G7 AI Action Plan, which promotes human-centered, secure, and trustworthy AI adoption across member economies. Organizations building frontier AI systems increasingly align with the Hiroshima Code as a signal of responsible development to regulators and enterprise customers.

## 9. UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence

| Field | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Version** | Adopted November 2021 (193 member states); monitoring framework deployed 2023–2025 |
| **Jurisdiction** | Global (UN member states) |
| **URL** | https://www.unesco.org/en/artificial-intelligence/recommendation-ethics |

The UNESCO Recommendation on AI Ethics is the first global standard on AI ethics, adopted unanimously by all 193 UNESCO member states in November 2021. It establishes a comprehensive normative framework covering 11 core principles — including human rights, fairness, transparency, sustainability, privacy, safety, and accountability — and provides detailed policy guidance across education, science, culture, communication, and governance. Unlike regulatory frameworks, it addresses the full societal and environmental footprint of AI, including ecological sustainability and digital divides. Member states committed to reporting on implementation through a Readiness Assessment Methodology (RAM). While voluntary and focused on principles rather than technical compliance, it is particularly influential in shaping AI policy in the Global South, where it often serves as the primary governance reference. The UNESCO framework is cited in national AI strategies across Africa, Latin America, and Southeast Asia, and is increasingly integrated into corporate AI ethics programs as a global normative baseline.

## 10. Singapore Model AI Governance Framework for Agentic AI

| Field | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Version** | MGF for Agentic AI (launched 22 January 2026); builds on MGF v2.0 (2020) |
| **Jurisdiction** | Singapore (voluntary; designed for global applicability) |
| **URL** | https://www.imda.gov.sg/resources/press-releases-factsheets-and-speeches/press-releases/2026/new-model-ai-governance-framework-for-agentic-ai |

Developed by Singapore's Infocomm Media Development Authority (IMDA) and launched at the World Economic Forum in January 2026, this is the world's first governance framework specifically designed for agentic AI — systems capable of autonomous planning, reasoning, and real-world action. It applies to all organizations deploying agentic AI in Singapore (including third-party agent users) and is organized around four dimensions: (1) assessing and bounding risks upfront, (2) ensuring meaningful human accountability, (3) implementing technical controls, and (4) enabling end-user responsibility. Compliance is voluntary, though organizations remain legally accountable for their agents' actions. The framework addresses emerging risks specific to agentic systems — including unauthorized actions, memory poisoning, privilege escalation, and cascading failures across multi-agent pipelines — that existing frameworks were not designed to handle. It complements Singapore's AI Verify testing toolkit and is shaping ASEAN-level governance norms. Singapore's National AI Council, established February 2026, will oversee implementation across priority sectors.

## 11. China AI Safety Governance Framework (Version 2.0)

| Field | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Version** | Version 2.0 (released 15 September 2025); Version 1.0 released September 2024 |
| **Jurisdiction** | China (non-binding technical guidance; underpins binding regulatory development) |
| **URL** | https://www.cac.gov.cn (Cyberspace Administration of China) |

Released at China's 2025 National Cybersecurity Awareness Week by the National Computer Network Emergency Response Technical Team (CNCERT) under the Cyberspace Administration of China (CAC), version 2.0 substantially expands the 2024 framework with more detailed risk classification, categorization, and enforcement guidance. It introduces a five-level risk grading system (Low, Moderate, Considerable, Major, Extremely Serious) based on intelligence level, application scenario, and scale of deployment, and assigns explicit safety responsibilities across four roles: developers, providers, users, and government authorities. Unlike NIST AI RMF, which remains voluntary in practice, China's framework is designed to rapidly feed into binding technical standards via TC260 (the National Information Security Standardization Technical Committee) and sector-specific regulations. A parallel "AI Safety Standards System v1.0" published in early 2025 mapped out the technical standards pipeline flowing from the 2024 framework, with standards on AI-generated content labeling already finalized. China is also exporting the framework internationally through the Global AI Governance Action Plan released at the 2025 World AI Conference.

## 12. South Korea AI Basic Act (Framework Act on AI Development and Establishment of a Foundation for Trustworthiness)

| Field | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Version** | Promulgated 21 January 2025; takes effect 22 January 2026 |
| **Jurisdiction** | Republic of Korea (with extraterritorial reach) |
| **URL** | https://aibasicact.kr |

South Korea's AI Basic Act is the first comprehensive national AI law in Asia-Pacific and the second in the world after the EU AI Act. Passed by the National Assembly in December 2024 and signed into law in January 2025, it consolidates 19 separate AI bills into a unified framework that both promotes AI development and establishes binding safety obligations. The law applies to organizations developing or deploying AI in South Korea and has extraterritorial reach for companies serving Korean users. Obligations include mandatory transparency requirements for high-impact and generative AI, risk assessment programs, safety measures, and appointment of a local representative. The Ministry of Science and ICT (MSIT) is the primary enforcement body, with an AI Safety Institute responsible for model evaluation and safety benchmarking. Implementation details are being finalized through Presidential Decrees, with a proposed three-year enforcement grace period under consideration. South Korea's approach uniquely combines promotional support — including AI data center investment, startup funding, and talent programs — with regulatory guardrails, aiming to position the country among the global top three AI powers by 2030.

## 13. BSI AI Cloud Services Criteria Catalogue (AIC4)

| Field | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Version** | AIC4 (current version; extension of C5:2020; C5:2026 in final publication) |
| **Jurisdiction** | Germany / European Union |
| **URL** | https://www.bsi.bund.de/EN/Themen/Unternehmen-und-Organisationen/Informationen-und-Empfehlungen/Kuenstliche-Intelligenz/AIC4/aic4_node.html |

The BSI Criteria Catalogue for AI Cloud Services (AIC4) is published by the German Federal Office for Information Security (Bundesamt für Sicherheit in der Informationstechnik) and specifies minimum information security requirements for machine learning systems deployed as cloud services. It operates as a formal extension of Germany's Cloud Computing Compliance Criteria Catalogue (C5), adding AI-specific security controls — covering training data integrity, model robustness, explainability, logging of model decisions, and adversarial attack resistance — to the existing cloud security baseline. Audits against AIC4 are conducted by qualified third-party auditors and result in attestation reports referenced by cloud customers in their own risk management programs. AIC4 has been cross-referenced in the CSA AI Controls Matrix and the EU AI Act's conformity assessment ecosystem. The parent C5 catalogue is undergoing a significant revision as C5:2026, published in final form by BSI in early 2026, with restructured criteria aligned to the European Union Agency for Cybersecurity (ENISA) Cloud Certification Scheme (EUCS), making AIC4 attestations more directly portable across the EU single market.

## 14. CSA AI Controls Matrix (AICM) Bundle

| Field | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Version** | AICM v1.0 Bundle (released July 2025) |
| **Jurisdiction** | International (Cloud Security Alliance member organizations) |
| **URL** | https://cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/working-groups/artificial-intelligence |

The Cloud Security Alliance (CSA) AI Controls Matrix is a comprehensive, vendor-agnostic security and governance toolkit specifically designed for generative AI systems, released in July 2025. The AICM Bundle includes the core controls matrix covering the full AI lifecycle (infrastructure through model output), an AI Consensus Assessment Initiative Questionnaire (AI-CAIQ) for self-assessment and third-party vendor evaluations, and detailed implementation guidelines with auditing best practices. It provides explicit crosswalk mappings to ISO/IEC 42001, ISO 27001, NIST AI RMF 1.0, BSI AIC4, and the EU AI Act, enabling organizations to satisfy multiple frameworks without duplicating evidence collection. The AICM is positioned as a cloud-first complement to the NIST AI RMF: while NIST extends a universal risk management structure into AI, CSA addresses the specific security challenges of shared-responsibility cloud environments hosting AI workloads. Adoption is growing among cloud service providers and enterprises seeking auditable AI security posture ahead of regulatory audits, particularly as the EU AI Act enforcement timelines activate.

*Note: Several additional ISO/IEC standards in the AI governance ecosystem are in active development or recently published, including ISO/IEC 23894:2023 (AI risk management guidance), ISO/IEC 22989:2022 (AI concepts and terminology), and IEEE 7000:2021 (ethically aligned system design). National frameworks from Japan (AI Safety Institute), India (National AI Mission Framework, expected 2026), and the UAE are also emerging but not yet at a sufficient maturity for certified compliance programs as of May 2026.*
